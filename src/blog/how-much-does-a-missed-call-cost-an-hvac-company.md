## The phone is still the front door

When an AC dies in July or a furnace quits in January, homeowners don't fill out a form and wait. They call, and if nobody picks up, they call the next company on the list. Urgent work like no cooling, no heat, breakdowns and replacements still comes in mostly by phone.

So take a shop that misses 10 sales calls a month. At roughly $1,200 each, that's $12,000 a month in opportunity. One caveat before going further: $1,200 is a benchmark, not a fact about your business. What a missed call is worth depends on your call mix, booking rate, average ticket and repeat customers. The point of this article is to help you swap it for your own number.

## What a missed call actually costs

A single missed call can carry four kinds of value:

1. **The service call itself:** the diagnostic and repair you would have run.
2. **The booked-job value:** the job value times the chance you'd have booked it.
3. **A replacement sale:** the occasional new system hiding inside a repair call.
4. **Future value:** the maintenance plan, the next tune-up, the referral.

## The benchmark: 27% of calls go unanswered

Call analysis widely attributed to Invoca puts the share of unanswered inbound calls to home-service companies at around 27%, across more than 60 million calls. That's about one in four. Treat it as an industry benchmark and check it against your own call log.

Misses bunch up in predictable places. Evenings and weekends, the lunch hour, heat waves and cold snaps when everyone calls at once, and any time your techs are out and the office is stretched.

## The quick math

Missed calls times $1,200. For a shop with 100 inbound calls a month:

- 100 inbound calls a month
- 27% unanswered is 27 missed calls
- 27 × $1,200 = $32,400 a month in opportunity
- Over a year, about $388,800

Call that revenue at risk, not lost cash. It assumes every missed call was a real sales call that would have booked at the benchmark value, which is generous.

## The better formula

Missed calls × booking rate × average job value = estimated lost revenue. Multiply that by your gross margin to get lost profit. Here it is with example inputs. Swap in your own.

| Step (example inputs) | Result |
| --- | --- |
| Missed calls a month | 27 |
| 70% are real sales opportunities | about 19 calls |
| 60% would have booked if answered | about 11 jobs |
| 9 service jobs × $450 | $4,050 |
| 2 replacements × $7,000 | $14,000 |
| Expected revenue a month | about $18,000 |
| Profit lost at a 35% gross margin | about $6,300 |

Three numbers, three meanings:

- **Revenue at risk:** $32,400 a month, the quick benchmark.
- **Expected revenue:** about $18,000 a month, the version you can defend.
- **Profit lost:** about $6,300 a month, what actually leaves your pocket.

Every input above is an example, not a statistic. The real number depends on your call mix, and even the careful version is large.

## Emergency calls are different

Count emergency calls on their own line. A no-heat call in a cold snap or a dead system in a heat wave books at a higher rate and often leads to a bigger ticket or a replacement. Published estimates for emergency calls are all over the place, so use your own history instead of anyone's average. ## Questions

### What counts as a missed call?

Any call from a customer or prospect that nobody answers live, or that's answered too late to win the job. Leave out vendors, spam and wrong numbers.

### Does a call that goes to voicemail count as missed?

For revenue, mostly yes. Plenty of homeowners with a broken AC or furnace won't leave a message. They call the next company. Count voicemail as missed unless you reliably call back within minutes.

### Is $1,200 profit or revenue?

It's a benchmark for expected revenue. It's not profit and it's not an invoice. Lost profit is that revenue times your gross margin, and your own number depends on call mix, booking rate, average ticket and repeat customers.
